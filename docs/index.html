Field checklist for suspicious labels

  • Does the hazard class / pictogram match the chemical (e.g. flammable for solvents, corrosive for acids)?
  • Is the CAS check digit valid, and does the CAS match the product name on the same label?
  • Does the company address, telephone and website exist and match the stated country of origin?
  • Lot/batch number, date and purity present? Printed professionally or stuck-on / hand-written?
  • Does the declared goods description and HS code on the shipping documents match the label?
  • Is the quantity or purity unusual for the declared use (e.g. drums of a reagent-grade lab chemical)?
  • For scheduled chemicals: ask for the import/export authorisation and end-user declaration.
  • A label is never proof of contents – confirm with a field test or laboratory analysis and secure samples under chain of custody.

How chemicals are controlled, from manufacture to importer

Scheduled precursors are controlled under Article 12 of the 1988 UN Convention. Each country applies it through national law, run by a Competent National Authority (CNA). The control chain below is the common pattern; exact permits and thresholds differ by country, so always confirm with the national law and the CNA.

  1. 1

    Manufacture and production

    The producer is licensed or registered with the national authority for the specific chemicals it makes. It keeps production and stock records, and must be able to account for losses.

    Documents: manufacturing licence / registration · production and stock records · safety data sheet and certificate of analysis (CoA).

  2. 2

    Storage, distribution and domestic sale

    Distributors and traders are licensed or registered. For every sale they identify the customer, record the quantity and stated end use, and report suspicious or unusual orders to the authority. Many countries require a customer declaration of legitimate use for sales of scheduled substances.

    Documents: trader licence / registration · customer declaration (end-use) · sales and stock records (the Convention requires records to be kept for at least 2 years) · suspicious-order reports.

  3. 3

    Export

    The exporter obtains an export authorisation for each shipment (or a general licence, depending on the country). The exporting authority issues a pre-export notification (PEN) to the importing country's authority, normally through the INCB's PEN Online system, and waits for a response or non-objection where the importing country requires one. Commercial documents must state the chemical's name as in the Convention table, the quantity, and the exporter, importer and consignee details, and containers must be labelled correctly.

    Documents: export authorisation · PEN reference / response · commercial invoice · packing list · transport document (bill of lading / airway bill) · customs export declaration · end-user declaration.

  4. 4

    Transit and transhipment

    Shipments passing through another country (or a free zone / transhipment hub) remain subject to customs supervision. Authorities may check the documents and, where national law allows, notify the origin and destination authorities. Consignments that change route, consignee or paperwork in transit are a key risk indicator.

    Documents: transit declaration · original transport documents · copies of the export authorisation and PEN where required.

  5. 5

    Import and customs clearance

    The importer needs an import authorisation or permit (where national law requires one) and must be a licensed/registered operator for that chemical. Customs matches the declaration, HS code and CAS number to the licence and the PEN, checks quantity and consignee, and may inspect and sample. A mismatch is grounds to hold the shipment under national law.

    Documents: import licence / permit · importer's operator licence · PEN notification on file · customs import declaration with correct HS code · invoice, packing list, transport document · CoA / SDS.

  6. 6

    Final user

    The importer supplies only registered or declared customers and keeps records of every transaction. Unusual quantities, new customers with no legitimate trade, or a stated use that does not fit the chemical should be reported to the authority.

    Documents: customer declarations · transaction records · reports of suspicious orders.

Which control applies?

StatusWhat to expect
Table IStrictest regime: licence or registration for manufacture and trade, export and import authorisation, pre-export notification, customer declarations, records and labelling. Salts and preparations are also controlled.
Table IISame framework, usually lighter: registration or licensing varies by country; pre-export notification where the importing country has asked for it; records and correct customs documents always apply. Many Table II chemicals are common industrial solvents and acids.
Not scheduledNo international licensing requirement. Some countries control them nationally, and the INCB runs voluntary monitoring. Treat unusual quantities or declared uses as intelligence indicators.

Regional and national examples: the EU applies Regulations 273/2004 (intra-EU trade) and 111/2005 (trade with non-EU countries), with licences, registrations, authorisations and customer declarations by category. The US applies the Controlled Substances Act (List I and II chemicals) through the DEA, with registration and import/export declarations. Check your own country's rules.

How to check a permit

  1. Identify the chemical. Use the Search tab: confirm name, CAS, HS code and whether it is Table I, Table II or not scheduled. Salts and preparations have different CAS and HS codes.
  2. Read the permit itself. Check the permit number, issuing authority, holder name and address, chemical name and CAS, quantity, validity dates, exporting and importing country, consignee, and whether it is for a single shipment or a period.
  3. Match it to the shipment. The holder must be the importer or exporter on the invoice and transport document. Name, CAS, HS code, quantity, packaging and container number must all agree. Quantity must not exceed the permit, and the permit must be valid on the date of import or export.
  4. Check the notification trail. For scheduled substances, ask your CNA whether a pre-export notification was sent for this shipment and whether the importing authority responded, using INCB PEN Online (access is for CNAs).
  5. Verify with the issuing authority. Do not rely on contact details printed on the permit. Look up the authority's contact through your CNA or the INCB list of CNAs, and ask them to confirm that the permit is genuine and still valid.
  6. Check the operator. Confirm the importer or exporter is registered or licensed for this chemical with the competent authority, not just that the company exists in a business register.
  7. If anything does not match, follow national law on holding, sampling and seizing; secure samples with chain of custody; and report through your CNA, which can raise it with the INCB Precursors Incident Communication System (PICS) and partner countries. See Intelligence & tools for PICS and the other reporting channels, and the Incident report sheet to collect the details.

Red flags

  • No permit, an expired permit, or one that does not name the chemical, quantity or consignee.
  • Permit number, stamp or signature that the issuing authority cannot confirm.
  • Chemical declared under another name or a wrong HS code, or declared as a mixture, "cleaning product" or "lab reagent" in industrial quantities.
  • New or recently registered company, no web presence, or a business unrelated to chemicals.
  • Illogical route or transhipment through several countries; last-minute change of consignee or destination.
  • Payment in cash or through third parties; pressure to clear quickly.
  • Labels whose CAS, UN number or manufacturer do not agree (use the Label check tab).
  • Quantities far above what the declared industry needs, or an end-use that does not fit the chemical.

Who to contact and where to look

  • Your Competent National Authority (CNA) for precursors: permit verification, PEN Online queries and incident reporting. Contacts are listed by the INCB: INCB CNA information.
  • INCB Precursors Control Section: incb.precursors@un.org · Red List.
  • UNODC multilingual dictionary of precursors and chemicals (names in many languages): UNODC.
  • Container Control Programme (CCP) / WCO and your regional customs liaison for intelligence sharing on suspicious containers.

    After an interdiction: a practical workflow

    1. Make the scene and the containers safe first. Do not open, smell or taste unknown chemicals. Use the PPE and procedures of your agency, keep people upwind, and keep oxidisers, acids, bases and flammable solvents apart. Call a chemical disposal or hazmat specialist for anything leaking, bulging or unidentified.
    2. Photograph before you touch. Labels, markings, lot numbers, seals, container and seal numbers, packaging, pallets and every transport and customs document. Photos are what other countries need to recognise a pattern.
    3. Identify. Search the name, CAS, UN or HS code on this page and run the Label check. Remember salts and preparations have different CAS numbers.
    4. Test and sample under chain of custody. A UNODC colour-test kit or Raman handheld gives a presumptive result only. Take representative samples, seal and record them, and send them to an authorised laboratory for confirmation.
    5. Check the paper trail. Ask your CNA whether an export or import authorisation and a pre-export notification (PEN) exist, and whether the issuing authority confirms them. See Controls & permits.
    6. Report and share. Use the Incident report sheet to collect the facts, then report through PICS (precursors and equipment), IONICS (fentanyl, NPS and related chemicals), CENcomm or ContainerCOMM (customs and container intelligence), and tell your Prism or Cohesion focal point.
    7. Trace back. Ask the manufacturer named on the label to confirm the lot and who bought it. Look at the supplier, the freight forwarder, the payment route and online postings. INCB publishes a guide for investigating suspicious internet postings of precursors.
    8. Look at the domestic side too. INCB has observed that diversion has become increasingly domestic: check storage, distribution and sale inside the country, not only the border.
    Registered government users

    PICSPrecursors Incident Communication System (INCB)

    INCB's secure online platform for real-time exchange between national authorities on incidents involving precursors, chemicals and equipment: seizures, shipments stopped in transit, attempted diversions, illicit laboratories and suspicious transactions.

    • Search incidents worldwide, set e-mail alerts, and attach photos and shipping documents.
    • INCB can put the authorities concerned in direct contact and issues special alerts through PICS.
    • Free to governments. Regulatory and law enforcement agencies request accounts by e-mail to incb.pics@un.org.

    Competent National Authorities

    PEN OnlinePre-Export Notification system (INCB)

    Launched in 2005. The exporting country's authority notifies the importing country's authority of a planned export of a Table I or II chemical, and the importing authority can answer with an objection or non-objection. It is the quickest way to confirm that a scheduled shipment was notified.

    • Your CNA registers and holds the access. Ask it to check the PEN trail for any scheduled consignment you stop.
    • Queries: incb.pen@un.org.

    PEN Online (UNODC)Find your CNA

    Governments · voluntary

    PEN Online LightTrade in non-scheduled chemicals (INCB)

    Launched in 2022 for the voluntary exchange of information on planned shipments of chemicals that are not under international control, most of them on the ISSL. It lets an importing country see legitimate trade in designer precursors and substitutes, and spot the shipment that does not fit.

    • In 2024 it tracked over 700 shipments of more than 136,000 tonnes and 3 million litres of non-controlled chemicals (INCB presentation).

    PEN Online Light brochure

    Governments only

    ISSL and equipment listSurveillance lists of non-scheduled chemicals and equipment (INCB)

    The limited International Special Surveillance List names non-scheduled chemicals known to be used in illicit drug manufacture, with recommended actions for authorities and industry. The 2026 edition adds precursors of fentanyl analogues and synthetic cannabinoids and excipients found in pre-mixed tableting powders. A companion International Monitoring List of Equipment (2nd edition, April 2026) covers machinery.

    • Reached through your CNA and the INCB secure portal. This tool does not reproduce the ISSL, so absence here is not clearance.

    Government focal points

    Project Prism and Project CohesionINCB global operational initiatives

    Networks of national focal points: Prism for precursors of synthetic drugs (amphetamine-type stimulants, fentanyl and others) and Cohesion for precursors of cocaine and heroin. Focal points receive special alerts and can join time-limited intelligence operations.

    • Example: Operation Pseudonym (2024) was a three-month operation that physically tracked PEN-notified shipments from source to final destination, focused on consignments above 25 kg.
    • To find or become a focal point: incb.precursors@un.org.
    Official government nomination

    GRIDS, IONICS, SNOOP, PINGGlobal Rapid Interdiction of Dangerous Substances (INCB)

    For fentanyl, nitazenes, other synthetic opioids and new psychoactive substances, and their chemicals, especially in post and express channels.

    • IONICS: secure incident communication and targeting platform, with daily automated notifications relevant to your country.
    • SNOOP: AI-assisted monitoring of online marketplaces for suspicious wholesale vendors.
    • PING: a pre-flight notification gateway that is still in development.

    European UnionRegulations (EC) 273/2004 and 111/2005

    273/2004 governs trade inside the EU and 111/2005 trade with non-EU countries. Category 1 substances need an operator licence; category 2 substances need registration (including users of subcategory 2A). The European database on drug precursors lists licensed and registered operators and is used to report seizures and stopped shipments.

    • To verify an EU operator, ask the competent authority of the member state, not the company.

    What this is
    
      
    A quick-reference tool for officers (customs, police, forensic and regulatory staff) to identify drug precursors and other chemicals frequently used in illicit drug manufacture, and to sanity-check what is printed on a container sticker.
    
      
    Scheduled chemicals: every substance in Tables I and II of the 1988 UN Convention as listed in the INCB Red List (23rd edition, July 2025), with CAS, HS code and – where one exists – UN transport number.
    
      
    Non-scheduled chemicals: substances widely reported as substitutes or reagents in clandestine laboratories (e.g. red phosphorus, iodine, benzyl cyanide, GBL, common solvents). The INCB's full limited International Special Surveillance List (ISSL) is restricted to governments and is not reproduced here.
    
      
    World map: for each country, the chemicals it controls nationally that are not in INCB Table I or II, taken from national legislation with the source linked. The INCB reports that about 50 countries control extra chemicals but publishes no per-country list, so coverage is graded verified (the full national list was read), partial (only part found or possibly out of date) or no public list found. Absence of data is not proof that a country has no extra controls. Corrections and new sources are welcome via data/countries.json in the repository.
    
      
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Limits you must know about
    
      
      
        
    • Salts, isomers and preparations of Table I/II substances are controlled but have different CAS numbers. A CAS that is not listed may still be a controlled salt – check other CAS and the INCB conversion table.
      • 
        
    • Many entries have no dedicated UN number because they are not dangerous goods in transport; a missing UN is not suspicious by itself.
      • 
        
    • The company list is small, indicative and not exhaustive. "Not in database" does not mean "fake", and "known producer" does not mean the product is genuine – counterfeit and re-labelled containers exist.
      • 
        
    • Legal status differs by country. Check national law (and regional rules, e.g. EU Regulations 273/2004 and 111/2005) before acting.
